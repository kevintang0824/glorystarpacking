Sheet construction

Treat basis weight and opacity as project variables.

The desired reveal depends on the actual product, sheet color, number of layers, artwork, lighting, and wrap. A basis-weight value by itself does not define opacity, softness, sound, drape, or tear behavior.

Flat sheet and wrapping method

Measure the approved fold, not a guessed sheet.

A sheet for lining a box, wrapping a product, interleaving components, or closing over the top follows a different geometry. Build a full-size mock-up and record the flat dimensions and fold sequence that produced it.

Coverage, fold bulk, exposed seams, opening sequence, print placement, product movement, and time or dexterity required for the intended workflow.
Box liner Internal box length, width and depth, side rise, corner treatment, edge reveal, product or insert footprint, and whether tissue sits above or below an insert. Corner bunching, interference with insert fit, surface marks, top-edge consistency, product loading, and the appearance when the lid opens.

Allowances are determined by the actual wrap, sheet behavior, product, and packing method. Record the approved flat size from a working mock-up instead of applying a universal overlap or tuck allowance.

Print coverage, color, and offset risk

Design for translucency and stacked sheets.

Ink is viewed through and against a thin base sheet, then multiplied where the tissue overlaps. Artwork that looks restrained on screen can become dense after folding, while heavy coverage can increase show-through, set-off, blocking, rub, and drying demands.

  • Map artwork to the foldShow the print origin, repeat, direction, seam, overlap, visible panels, and areas hidden under the product or box edge on a full-size dieline.
  • Define color by substrateUse a physical reference and judge the ink over the selected base-sheet color; do not expect screen values or a box proof to predict translucent tissue.
  • Control coverage intentionallyCompare motif density, line weight, solids, negative space, overlapping layers, and whether a print-free product-contact area is required by the project.
  • Review both sidesCheck the intended face and the reverse appearance, because show-through and print penetration can affect orientation, folding, and presentation.
  • Approve after holdingFold, stack, bundle, and pack printed sheets under agreed pressure and conditions, then review ink transfer, sticking, rub, odor concerns, and color change after the defined period.

If the tissue will touch food, cosmetics, delicate finishes, textiles, or another regulated or sensitive product, identify the contact conditions and destination requirements before materials and inks are selected. Suitability must be reviewed for that exact use.

Show-through and transfer review

Test the risk in the finished stack and wrap.

Show-through is a visual relationship among sheet, ink, product, layers, folds, and lighting. Offset is a transfer relationship influenced by ink, coverage, drying, pressure, temperature, humidity, storage, and contact time.

Project checks for printed tissue appearance and contact between sheets.
Condition Potential concern Physical check
Dark product under light tissue Product color or graphics may dominate the sheet, alter the apparent ink color, or reduce the intended concealment. Place one and the intended number of layers over the actual product and view flat, folded, and inside the final box under agreed lighting.
Dark or dense print Reverse show-through, uneven visual density at overlaps, transfer to adjacent sheets, rub, and a heavier hand feel in printed areas. Compare representative coverage patterns on the selected sheet, then fold, stack, hold, separate, rub, and inspect both faces and contact surfaces.
Multiple folded layers Ink and base color can build at overlaps, artwork can misalign at the reveal, and fold bulk can change closure or product fit. Use the production-intent folding sequence and count; inspect the closed presentation, opening reveal, corners, seams, and clearance.
Long stacked storage Pressure, heat, humidity, and time may change blocking, set-off, curl, odor, or ease of sheet separation. Agree a representative packed holding condition, then inspect bundle separation, transfer, flatness, appearance, and packing-line handling.
Contact with finished product Ink, coating, fiber, dust, moisture, or abrasion may interact with a sensitive surface or transfer to the product. Use representative product materials and the intended contact duration and environment; define acceptance with the buyer's product and compliance teams.
Mixed artwork or colors Wrong sheets, cross-transfer, bundle confusion, mismatched counts, or incorrect presentation can enter a SKU kit. Define separators, bundle identity, color or SKU reconciliation, mixed-pack rules, carton marks, and line-clearance checks.

Box and bag coordination

Coordinate a system, not identical ink recipes.

Translucent tissue, coated box wrap, uncoated bag paper, ribbon, labels, and foil reflect and absorb light differently. The same nominal color value can produce visibly different results across those materials.

  • Nominate one physical master reference and identify the lighting used for approval
  • Define whether each component should be a close match, lighter or darker tone, or deliberate contrast
  • Review the tissue both as a single sheet and in the actual folded layers over the product
  • Place the tissue, box, bag, label, ribbon, and other components together before final approval
  • Record separate material and color references for every component in the packaging specification

A coordinated packaging set can be more repeatable when the tissue is intentionally related to the box and bag rather than treated as an exact match across fundamentally different surfaces.

Folding and pack-out

Specify how sheets arrive at the packing station.

The flat sheet size is only part of the supply format. Fold count, fold direction, printed face, bundle quantity, compression, protection, and carton orientation determine whether tissue can be received, separated, and applied consistently.

  • Lock the fold mapShow each fold in order, finished folded dimensions, printed face, opening edge, logo direction, and whether sheets are nested, interleaved, or individually folded.
  • Set usable bundle countsDefine sheets per bundle, bundles per carton, separators, banding or bagging if used, mixed-design rules, and how partial quantities are identified.
  • Protect without over-compressingReview carton fit, stacking direction, liner, moisture conditions, edge protection, pressure, and the risk of crease polishing, blocking, curl, or crushed folds.
  • Plan receiving dataRecord sheet size, artwork or color, revision, quantity, batch or other agreed identification, carton marks, carton dimensions, and gross weight.

Ask the packer to trial a production-intent bundle. A fold that looks clean as a single hand sample may separate slowly, open in the wrong direction, or place the printed face incorrectly when supplied in bulk.

Sampling and quality control

Approve the sheet, wrap, and bundle together.

Acceptance criteria should point to released artwork, a documented sheet construction, a full-size printed sample, the approved wrapping mock-up, fold map, bundle method, and agreed inspection approach.

01 · Wrap brief

Lock use and geometry

Confirm product and pack dimensions, contact conditions, flat sheet size, artwork direction, wrapping sequence, overlaps, tucks, folds, and sheets per product.

02 · Printed sample

Judge the actual sheet

Review basis-weight and opacity direction, base color, surface, print coverage, ink color, reverse appearance, trim, dimensions, folds, and presentation.

03 · Pack-out trial

Fold, hold, and open

Wrap representative products, stack and bundle sheets, apply agreed pressure and conditions, then check show-through, offset, blocking, rub, curl, separation, and line handling.

04 · Production QC

Inspect and identify

Check against agreed criteria for material, color, print, dimensions, orientation, folds, sheet and bundle counts, design identity, protection, carton marks, and packing.

A digital proof can approve content, repeat, and general layout, but it cannot establish tissue opacity, drape, fold memory, reverse show-through, ink transfer, sheet separation, or the appearance of overlapping layers.

Recycling and material claims

Write the claim for the exact sheet and destination.

End-of-life language is not a generic material adjective. Collection, sorting, local acceptance, sheet construction, inks, coatings, additives, contamination, and use can all change whether a claim is supportable.

Inputs to review before placing a recycling or material claim on packaging or marketing.
Claim input Question to document Control before release
Exact construction Which paper, fiber claim, recycled-content claim, colorants, inks, coatings, treatments, additives, adhesives, or seals are in the supplied sheet? Keep supplier documentation tied to the approved material code and artwork revision; do not transfer evidence from a different tissue construction.
Destination In which countries, regions, municipalities, or named recovery programs will the claim appear, and what rules or guidance apply there? Review wording market by market with current local requirements and avoid implying universal collection or acceptance.
Use and contamination Will the tissue contact food, cosmetics, oils, adhesives, glitter, product residue, or other materials that may affect collection or processing? Describe disposal only for the intended use and identify exclusions or conditions when they are needed for an accurate claim.
Consumer action Does the instruction require separation from a sticker, ribbon, tape, label, laminate, or another packaging component before disposal? Make the instruction clear, feasible, and consistent with the physical pack and the local program being referenced.
Evidence and wording What test, certification, supplier declaration, calculation, legal review, or program guidance supports the precise statement and any qualification? Match the claim's scope to the evidence, retain the approval record, and recheck it when the material, artwork, market, or rule changes.

“Paper,” “recycled,” and “recyclable” describe different attributes. None should be treated as proof of another, and local collection or acceptance should not be implied without destination-specific support.
