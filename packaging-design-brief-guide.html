Write the packaging design brief before choosing the prettiest concept.

Define what the package must communicate, contain, survive, and prove so designers, reviewers, and manufacturers solve the same problem.

Short answer

A useful packaging design brief makes subjective choices testable.

Before requesting concepts, write down who is buying, where the package will be seen, what the product is, what the package must do, which statements are required, which claims are approved, what cannot change, and who has authority to approve each decision. Attach the real product facts, container or box dimensions, channel requirements, structural assumptions, production constraints, artwork assets, timing, quantity range, and deliverables.

Then define how concepts will be compared. Mandatory legal, structural, barcode, and production requirements are pass-or-fail gates. Message clarity, buyer relevance, differentiation, system flexibility, and visual character can be weighted. Review at actual size and on the intended structure before production release.

Buyer rule: do not ask a large audience to choose between concepts until the project team agrees what a winning package must accomplish. A vote can express preference; it cannot supply the missing strategy, regulatory review, structure, or print specification.

Current buyer signal

A Reddit founder had seven label directions but no decision frame.

The discussion reveals a live planning problem. It does not prove that one aesthetic, message, or layout works for every product.

In an r/smallbusiness discussion posted September 13, 2026, a first-time food founder shared seven very different AI-generated label concepts and asked which was strongest. The post also said the text should be ignored and the brand name was not final. Replies pushed the decision upstream: clarify the audience and brand direction, and decide how the unfamiliar product should be explained before selecting a visual route.

The thread was reviewed September 29, 2026. It is a buyer-demand signal, not design research or a compliance source. The practical lesson is not that crowds should never review packaging. It is that feedback becomes more useful when reviewers see the same product facts, target context, required copy, concept objective, and evaluation questions.

Message before moodboard

Write the communication hierarchy in plain language.

A concept should solve a named communication problem, not merely resemble a reference.

  1. Recognition: what must a shopper understand first at the expected viewing distance or thumbnail size?
  2. Identification: which exact product name, variant, quantity, or model prevents the wrong choice?
  3. Relevance: what approved sentence explains why this product belongs in the buyer's situation?
  4. Proof: which substantiated facts can support the promise, and who approved them?
  5. Action: what should the buyer do next—compare, open, scan, prepare, store, reuse, or reorder?

Do not ask the designer to invent regulated copy, technical performance, certification status, country-of-origin wording, environmental claims, or product benefits. Assign an owner to provide and approve each statement. If a fact is still unknown, mark it as an open item with a due date rather than filling the panel with placeholder certainty.

For U.S. food packaging, the FDA's Food Labeling Guide explains the principal display panel and information panel framework, while the FDA notes that parts of the guide are being revised and points businesses to newer allergen and Nutrition Facts resources. Use the rules for the actual category and market; a general design brief is not a compliance review.

Review in the right sequence

Move from strategy to a released production file through six gates.

Each gate answers a different question; combining them makes feedback noisy.

  1. Brief approval: product, audience, package job, message hierarchy, markets, constraints, ownership, schedule, and deliverables are agreed.
  2. Route review: compare genuinely different strategic directions using rough concepts; do not spend production-artwork time on every option.
  3. System stress test: apply the preferred route to key SKUs, long and short copy, required panels, identifiers, and future variants.
  4. Structural alignment: place the chosen system on the supplier's current dieline and confirm panels, folds, seams, openings, inserts, and usable print areas.
  5. Physical proof: print at actual size, wrap or assemble it on the intended structure, check hierarchy, legibility, surfaces, opening sequence, and pack-out. Use the packaging sample approval checklist for the physical review.
  6. Production release: preflight the controlled artwork, confirm approvals, record the version, and issue the supplier package described in the dieline and artwork requirements guide.

Barcode and name checks

Reserve evidence-dependent elements before the layout hardens.

Identifiers and names have workflows of their own; they are not last-minute decoration.

GS1's General Specifications include barcode symbol placement rules by package and scanning environment. The correct symbol, size, orientation, print quality, data, and verification plan depend on the actual use. Put a barcode requirement in the brief, reserve a realistic area, and have the responsible party verify the final printed result. A decorative placeholder barcode in a render is not approval evidence.

If a brand or product name is not final, label it as provisional. In the United States, the USPTO trademark search resources explain why similar marks and coordinated classes matter, but a database search alone is not a complete clearance opinion. The buyer should assign name clearance and usage approval to a qualified owner before printing inventory.

Supplier-ready handoff

Convert the chosen design into a manufacturable request.

The design brief remains the intent record; the RFQ and specification control what is being bought.

  • Approved brand assets, copy deck, claims, product data, identifiers, language versions, and named approvers.
  • Representative product or accurate product envelope, primary container, orientation, weight, sensitivity, pack-out, and distribution route.
  • Chosen package type, current dieline owner and version, finished dimensions, material assumptions, print process, colors, finishes, and tolerances to confirm.
  • SKU list, quantities by version, target markets, sample stages, test plan, quality references, packing method, delivery destination, and required date.
  • Deliverables: editable masters, linked assets, production PDFs, color and finish separations, mockups, revision log, proof records, and released version.
  • Open questions with owners and due dates; do not silently convert assumptions into specifications.

Use the custom packaging RFQ template to compare manufacturing scope, then qualify the responder with the supplier qualification checklist. For a family of products, connect the brief to the multi-SKU packaging system guide.

Sources and method

Use community discussion to find the question and official sources to define controlled requirements.

This guide used the September 13, 2026 r/smallbusiness discussion as a current buyer signal. Regulatory and identifier examples were checked against the FDA Food Labeling Guide and its update notices, the GS1 General Specifications, and the USPTO trademark search resources. Sources were reviewed September 29, 2026.

Requirements vary by product category, market, channel, package type, retailer, claim, and current law or standard. This guide is a planning workflow, not legal advice, trademark clearance, regulatory approval, structural engineering, barcode verification, or a production guarantee. Assign qualified reviewers and recheck current primary sources for the actual project.
