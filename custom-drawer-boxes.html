Sleeve-and-tray anatomy

Three interfaces determine the experience.

The sleeve-to-tray fit, the tray-to-insert fit, and the user-to-pull interaction need to work as one system.

Selection logic

Define how far the product needs to travel.

A drawer should expose the product clearly without asking the user to overextend a loaded tray. The required travel distance follows the insert layout and removal sequence.

  • One-end or two-end opening and the direction the artwork should face
  • Product reveal point, removal clearance, tray weight, and center of gravity
  • Need for a pull ribbon, finger notch, thumb cut, or exposed tray edge
  • Retention approach if the tray should resist accidental full withdrawal

Friction and tolerances

A clean slide comes from controlled buildup.

Nominal dimensions are only the start. Board, paper, seams, adhesive, finish, and squareness all change the space between sleeve and tray.

Clearance

Leave room for real materials

The engineering allowance must account for the finished tray, wrapped sleeve, seam positions, and normal variation without creating visible looseness.

Surface contact

Review the rubbing faces

Paper texture, lamination, foil edges, embossing, and glue areas should be kept away from critical sliding surfaces where practical.

Physical sample

Approve effort, not a number

The sample should be loaded with the intended product so opening effort, tray stability, travel, and product access can be judged together.

Board and wrap

Specify the moving surfaces deliberately.

Material choice affects rigidity, corner definition, print appearance, finish registration, and the friction between the two structural parts.

Core board

Thickness selected around span and product load

Uncoated wrap

Texture and fold behavior checked in sample

Dark wrap

Edge treatment and surface marking reviewed

Focal finish

Kept clear of functional sliding zones

Tray insert

The insert must protect without bowing the tray.

Insert geometry begins with the product, then considers removal access, tray wall support, accessory layout, and the direction the packed box will travel.

  • Product dimensions, weight, fragile areas, orientation, and acceptable contact points
  • Finger access, pull tabs, ribbons, documentation wells, and accessory compartments
  • Paperboard, molded fiber, or foam concepts reviewed against the project brief
  • Clearance from tray walls so the insert does not increase friction inside the sleeve

QC and carton orientation

Check the slide, then protect it in transit.

Inspection criteria follow the approved sample and documented specification. Final packing should keep the loaded tray from working against the sleeve opening during handling.

  • Sleeve and tray dimensions, squareness, seams, edge condition, and visible alignment
  • Start friction, continuous slide, reveal point, product access, and unintended tray release
  • Insert seating, product retention, pull attachment, notch finish, and artwork direction
  • Unit protection, master-carton orientation, dividers if required, carton count, and shipping marks

Drawer box questions

Resolve the moving parts before artwork approval.

A drawer box combines an outer sleeve with an inner tray that slides out from one or both ends. The tray carries the product and insert, while the sleeve guides travel, protects the contents, and provides the main exterior branding surface.
A pull ribbon adds a separate tactile cue and can suit a formal reveal, but its fixing point and storage position need to be controlled. A finger notch is simpler and keeps the access feature within the board structure. The right choice follows tray weight, opening direction, user grip, and visual priority.
Travel should expose enough of the insert for the product to be seen and removed without forcing the tray beyond a comfortable handling position. Product orientation, insert access, tray weight, and any retention feature are checked in the structural sample.
Friction is affected by sleeve and tray dimensions, board thickness, wrap buildup, seams, corner squareness, surface finish, and production tolerances. The sample should use representative materials so the sliding action can be approved physically.
Yes. The insert can organize one product or a set, but it must leave access for removal and must not distort the tray walls. Product dimensions, weight, fragile areas, presentation angle, and shipping orientation guide the insert construction.
